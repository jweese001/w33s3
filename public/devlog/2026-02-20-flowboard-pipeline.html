Exploring Consistency in AI Image Generation

February 20, 2026

For several months now, I have been working on a pipeline that generates illustrated storyboards from screenplays. Over the past two weeks, I pushed through four complete stories — 226 images total — to stress-test the workflow and figure out where it breaks.

The question I keep returning to: can reference images, used systematically, reduce the manual correction burden? Not eliminate it. Just make it manageable enough that generating a 50-shot story feels like work rather than punishment.

The Consistency Problem

This is well-trodden ground. AI image generators have no memory between calls. Your character in scene 1 bears little resemblance to the same character in scene 5. The same room looks completely different from two camera angles. Everyone who has tried to generate a sequence of related images knows this.

Reference images are the standard mitigation — you pass in an example image alongside your prompt, and the model uses it as a guide. Nothing novel there. What I have been testing is whether a systematic approach to reference management, built into a repeatable workflow, changes the economics of correction.

The Setup

FlowBoard is a node-based tool I have been building for this. You wire together reusable components — characters, settings, styles, camera setups — into shot definitions. The tool assembles prompts from the graph and handles the API calls to Gemini.

Before generating any story shots, I generate character portraits. These become the visual anchor. When a character appears in a scene, their portrait gets sent to Gemini as a reference image.

Character portraits for "Damon and the Shotgun." These get wired to every scene where the character appears.

One detail that seemed to matter: wiring the portrait to both the character node and the output node. Without the second connection, Gemini often ignores the reference and invents its own interpretation. With it, the model pays more attention. I cannot say why — just that it produced noticeably better results in my testing.

What I Noticed

After 226 images across four stories, some patterns emerged.

Character consistency improved significantly. Not perfect — Gemini still drifts, especially in wide shots with multiple characters — but the portraits reduced the most egregious mismatches. Mason looks recognizably like Mason across most shots. That was not true before I started using reference images systematically.

First-pass generation is fast. 49 shots in about 35 minutes. The workflow removes the friction of writing prompts from scratch each time. Whether that speed is worth the setup cost depends on how many images you are generating.

Corrections are still the real work. The first pass gets you maybe 70% of the way there. The remaining 30% — fixing expressions, removing props that bled into wrong scenes, adjusting spatial relationships — takes longer than the initial generation. Some shots took 8 iterations to get right.

"Damon and the Shotgun" — the inciting incident. Portrait reference kept his appearance consistent, but the shotgun bled into unrelated scenes throughout the story.

What Works, What Does Not

What Seems to Help

  • Portrait references — character faces and clothing stay more consistent
  • Minimal text descriptions — "use the reference image" works better than detailed descriptions that contradict the portrait
  • Negative prompts — blocking text, logos, and speech bubbles prevents most unwanted additions
  • Cross-scene editing — using a good shot as reference to fix a bad one
  • Contact sheets for review — seeing all shots at once makes problems obvious

What Remains Difficult

  • Spatial consistency — the same room looks different from different angles
  • Prop bleeding — dominant props (a shotgun, in one story) leak into unrelated scenes
  • Expression calibration — Gemini interprets emotions at maximum intensity
  • Multi-character wide shots — 4+ people at distance rarely comes out right
  • Precise positioning — placing an element at a specific size and angle is tedious

The Most Useful Correction Pattern

When a shot needed fixing, the most effective technique was using another shot as reference. If D8-2 had the right prop setup (cloth under the shotgun, scattered shells), I could fix D8-1 by sending D8-2 as reference with the instruction "add the cloth and shells from the second image."

This shot became the "source of truth" for prop continuity in related scenes.

Teaching by example works better than teaching by description. "Make this look like that" is more reliable than "add a cloth under the shotgun and scatter some shells around it." This observation led me to wonder: what if I generated reference images specifically for this purpose, before starting the first pass?

The Current Experiment: Setting References

If character portraits help with people, maybe location references would help with spaces. The hypothesis: pre-generating establishing shots of each setting — empty rooms with key props and architectural details — and wiring them to all shots in that scene might reduce the spatial inconsistency problem.

"Courtroom Collapse" — 48 shots in the same courtroom. Does Gemini understand it is the same room from different angles?

I ran an initial test today with a conference room scene. Generated two clean setting references — one wide establishing shot from the doorway, one lower angle from the table — and wired them to a couple of test shots. Early results suggest it helps. The room maintained its glass walls and general layout across different camera angles.

But there are open questions:

  • Does Gemini reason about spatial relationships, or just blend? When I give it two angles of the same room, does it understand they show the same space from different positions? Or does it average them into something incoherent?
  • How many setting references are too many? The API accepts up to 14 reference images. Character portraits already consume some of that budget. At what point does adding more references degrade rather than improve results?
  • What should setting references contain? My initial tests used empty rooms with props. But maybe including a character in one reference helps establish scale. I do not know yet.

The test case is the courtroom from "Courtroom Collapse" — 48 shots in a single location with well-defined architectural features. If setting references can maintain consistency there, the technique is probably worth the setup cost. If the courtroom still looks different in every shot, I will need to rethink the approach.

What Would Success Look Like?

I am not trying to eliminate corrections entirely. That seems unrealistic given the current state of the technology. The goal is to shift the ratio — less time fixing, more time generating.

Concretely: if I can get a first pass where 80-85% of shots are usable with minor tweaks, and only 15-20% need significant rework, that feels sustainable for longer projects. Right now I am closer to 70/30, which is workable but tedious.

The other success criterion is consistency across shots that are supposed to match. A character's outfit should not change between scenes. A room should be recognizable from multiple angles. These are the failures that break the illusion of a coherent visual story.

"Accidental Frontman" — four characters, consistent outfits, correct instruments. One of the harder shots to get right.

What I Expect to Be Hard

Multi-character wide shots will probably remain difficult. Gemini struggles with complex spatial arrangements — who is standing where, which direction they face, what instruments they hold. Portrait references help with faces but do not solve positioning.

I also expect diminishing returns from adding more references. At some point, the model has too much conflicting information and produces worse results than with fewer constraints. Finding that threshold will take more experimentation.

The deeper issue is that these models do not have a persistent understanding of the world they are depicting. Each generation is stateless. References are a workaround, not a solution. Until that changes at the model level, we are working around a fundamental limitation.

But workarounds that reduce the correction burden by even 20-30% make a meaningful difference when you are generating hundreds of images. That is what I am trying to find.
