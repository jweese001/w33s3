Adding a 3D Block-In Layer to FlowBoard

July 12, 2026

This week I worked on FlowBoard, but not on the part most people would notice first. Instead of a flashy front-end change, I spent my time adding a new skill to the FlowBoard toolchain: flowboard-3d-fx. The goal is to make it much easier to build rough Three.js scene block-ins that can feed FlowBoard's Scene and FX nodes as visual reference.

The bigger idea here is that AI image workflows usually get more reliable when I can stop describing every shot in pure text. Sometimes I do not want to jump straight from screenplay language to image generation and hope the composition lands. I want a lightweight 3D staging layer first: camera placement, silhouette, lighting direction, and spatial relationships. This week was about turning that into a repeatable skill instead of an ad hoc manual process.

The hard part was not Three.js

The interesting technical problem was not “can I generate a scene in Three.js?” It was “can I generate a scene that satisfies the exact contract FlowBoard expects when it captures from the browser IDE?” That contract turned out to be stricter than it looks. A scene can run fine in the editor and still fail silently when FlowBoard tries to capture it at the requested resolution or restore camera presets.

So the new skill is built around that constraint. I documented the scene contract, added a validator in scripts/validate.py, and made the workflow insist on exposing the right window globals like window.renderer, window.scene, window.camera, and window.controls. Those are small details, but they are exactly the kind of details that separate “it renders on my machine” from “it plugs into the product correctly.”

Adding guardrails around the workflow

I also added a share-URL generator and a live browser test. The share URL matters because the handoff into the Three.js IDE should feel simple: generate a scene, open it in the IDE, paste or load it, and capture. The live test matters because validators only catch structural mistakes. I wanted another layer that boots a real browser, runs the scene, exercises the capture flow, and proves the FlowBoard-side protocol still works end to end.

That combination changed how I think about this feature. The skill is not just “write some scene code.” It is really a small reliability system: authoring guidance, contract documentation, mechanical validation, browser-level verification, and then an example scene that shows the standard in practice. I capped the week off by adding a noir alley example and tightening the README so the whole FlowBoard skills package explains where this new piece fits.

Why this feels useful

I like this direction because it gives FlowBoard a better bridge between story intent and image generation. A screenplay shot like “wide exterior, low angle, dusk, subject framed against signage” can now become a composition block-in instead of only a paragraph of prompt text. That is a more concrete artifact. It is easier to inspect, easier to revise, and easier to reuse.

Next I want to see how far this can go in real production use. The immediate question is whether these block-ins consistently improve shot planning for narrative workflows, or whether I still need better tooling around camera presets, reusable staging kits, and FX-node handoff. Either way, this week felt like solid product work: not just adding another feature, but making a messy creative step more testable and more repeatable.
